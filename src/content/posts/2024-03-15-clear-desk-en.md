A desk attracts objects with surprising ease. Unfinished mail, charging cables, receipts, and empty cups arrive one by one until, before I can begin working, I have to move everything out of the way. Each interruption seems minor, but repeated every day, it takes a real toll on the effort required to focus.

So I decided to keep only what I need for the current task on my desk. When I write, a laptop, a notebook, and a single pen are enough. Everything else has a designated place in a drawer within reach.

![An open workspace with only a laptop, notebook, and pen](/assets/images/posts/clear-desk/clear-workspace.webp)

## Give things a place instead of merely clearing them away

My desk never stayed organized because I had only moved things somewhere else temporarily. Anything without a place of its own found its way back to the desk a few days later.

I assigned chargers to a small box inside a drawer, mail that needed attention to a single vertical file, and cups to the kitchen as soon as I finished working. Frequently used objects should be accessible in one motion; things used only occasionally can remain out of sight.

A clear desk does not guarantee productivity. But once I know what I need to do, having less information in view makes the first step easier. There is less chance of something else catching my attention between opening the laptop and opening the document.

## A three-minute end to the day

![Putting away a notebook and pen at the end of the day](/assets/images/posts/clear-desk/evening-reset.webp)

When I finish working, I spend three minutes returning the desk to its original state. I close the notebook, put the pen in its tray, and take away the cup. I write tomorrow’s task on a sheet of paper and tuck it inside the notebook.

This brief reset has become a small ritual separating today’s work from tomorrow’s. When I face an empty desk in the morning, I can begin with the first task of the day instead of clearing away traces of the day before.

The desk does not always have to be perfectly bare. Sometimes I leave out a project that will continue for several days or a book I am still reading. The important question is not whether the desk looks clean enough for a photograph, but whether the objects on it are connected to what currently holds my attention.

Open space on a desk is not space where nothing happens. It is room left for thinking, working with my hands, and beginning whatever comes next.
