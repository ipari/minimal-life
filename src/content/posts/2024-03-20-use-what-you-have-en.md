When I look at new products, the small flaws in what I already own suddenly seem much larger. After reading that something is lighter, faster, or more convenient, even a perfectly functional object can begin to feel outdated.

Instead of replacing it, I first ask whether it still does what I need it to do. Does the bag carry what I need? Are the shoes comfortable to walk in? Does the bottle keep water from leaking? If the answer is yes, the appeal of a new feature is usually closer to curiosity than necessity.

## Distinguish wear from failure

Things show signs of age. Fabric fades, metal picks up small scratches, and leather softens where it is often touched. These changes do not prevent an object from working. They are traces of the time it has spent adapting to my life.

![Repairing a worn area on a well-used bag by hand](/assets/images/posts/use-what-you-have/repair.webp)

Damage, on the other hand, deserves attention. If a zipper catches or a handle comes loose, I check whether the affected part can be repaired before discarding the whole object. A few stitches or a tightened screw can sometimes add years to its life.

There are times when a repair costs nearly as much as a replacement. In those cases, I consider more than price: familiarity, waste, and the time required to find something new all matter. The goal is not to repair everything at any cost. It is simply to avoid deciding too quickly that replacement is the only option.

## What it means to use something fully

![Everyday objects that show their age but remain well cared for](/assets/images/posts/use-what-you-have/well-used-things.webp)

Using something fully does not mean forcing myself to keep it until it breaks. If it no longer suits me or I no longer need it, passing it on while it is still in good condition may be better. An object continues its useful life when it reaches someone who needs it.

With consumables, I check what remains before buying more. I use a notebook through its final page, finish cosmetics before their expiration dates, and cook with what is already in the refrigerator before looking at the next grocery promotion. These are small habits, but they prevent a surprising number of duplicate purchases.

This practice has also changed how I choose things. I now look past impressive features and ask whether the construction is simple, replacement parts are available, and cleaning and repair are straightforward. Even if the initial price is higher, an object that lasts can cost less over its lifetime.

Using what I already have is less an exercise in resisting consumption than a way of deepening a relationship. As I care for familiar objects and put them to use again, my sense of what I truly need becomes clearer.
